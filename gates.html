Making design buildable

Introduced mandatory design–development gates to surface technical feasibility, content, and delivery risks before they became expensive downstream rework.

01

Outcomes

  • Reduced downstream rework

    Identified feasibility issues before design commitments translated into expensive development changes.

  • Improved delivery predictability

    Surfaced technical dependencies and implementation risks earlier, reducing unexpected disruptions to committed timelines.

  • Cleaner design–development handoffs

    Reduced ambiguity through clearer specifications, documented edge cases, and structured engineering acceptance.

  • Better protection of project margins

    Reduced exposure to unplanned, unbillable development effort caused by feasibility gaps and incomplete handoffs.

02

The problem

Design and development operated as separate functions. Designs were presented to clients, approved, and only then introduced to engineering. By that point, feasibility concerns could no longer influence decisions without disrupting commitments.

Handoff had become a file-sharing exercise rather than a delivery milestone. Copy often arrived after UI development began, and real content broke layouts built around placeholders. The resulting rework was rarely billable, directly affecting project margins.

The disconnect was cultural as well as operational.

The underlying problem was systemic: critical delivery decisions depended on individual experience and project-specific habits, with no consistent mechanism to validate feasibility, manage dependencies, or establish readiness.

03

The gates

I redefined handoff as a governed delivery milestone, not the moment design shared its files.

Each gate was positioned at a point where feedback could still change the outcome without incurring significant rework. Project managers were accountable for adherence, and I joined internal kickoffs for design–development projects to establish expectations and walk teams through the process.

Discovery1
Gate 1

Feature feasibility

At the end of discovery, development reviews the information architecture and feature list to identify technical constraints and dependencies before scope is committed.

Wireframes5
Gate 5Starts here, runs to release

Content readiness

Real copy is introduced at the wireframe stage wherever possible. Unresolved content is treated as a delivery risk, not silently passed to development.

Why content is a gate. Copy that arrives after the UI is finished breaks things both ways. When the client’s final copy doesn’t fit the placeholder components, approved layouts break and have to be redesigned and rebuilt. When it’s forced to fit, writers squeeze the message into fixed boxes, leaving cramped layouts and truncated text. So I moved content finalisation upstream to wireframing. When content stayed unresolved, PMs flagged its impact on timeline and scope to the client.

Gate 5 runs to release

Gate 2The questions the design feasibility review asks

  • Can this be implemented within the agreed scope and timeline?
  • Can an existing component or library achieve the same design intent?
  • Do complex interactions, animations, or custom widgets introduce disproportionate build effort?
  • Which technical decisions or dependencies must be resolved before design is committed?

The objective wasn’t to reduce design ambition. It was to preserve the intended experience while making implementation effort, trade-offs, and risks visible before commitments were made.

An added benefit. Even before a developer is formally allocated to the build, early visibility into the designs gives the development team a mental model of the work ahead.
04

The handoff checklist

I operationalised Gate 3 through a shared handoff template that every project copied. It replaced subjective judgments of readiness with explicit documentation and engineering acceptance.

Knowledge-transfer calls were recorded and saved to a shared drive. Developers documented edge cases and open questions directly in the design file, keeping context connected to the work.

05

Making it stick

  1. 1

    Pilot first.

    Ran the gates on four projects, gathered feedback on overhead and friction, and refined the process before rolling it out studio-wide.

  2. 2

    Explain the why, then hold the line.

    When teams began bypassing design feasibility reviews, I reinforced adherence through PM communication grounded in actual examples of the risks those reviews prevented. Scheduling joint reviews became an explicit PM responsibility.

  3. 3

    Make review easy.

    Introduced a dedicated channel for screen-review requests, reducing the coordination overhead of engaging development for a feasibility check.

  4. 4

    Learn on purpose.

    Established the studio’s first retrospectives on development projects. PMs captured weekly observations across scope changes, estimation gaps, handoff friction, resourcing, sales support, context gaps, and gate adherence to identify recurring patterns and opportunities for improvement.

06

The operating model

I structured the workflow around five interconnected principles:

  1. Prevent

    Identify feasibility, content, and dependency risks before they become expensive delivery problems.

  2. Standardise

    Define what good handoff and design QA look like through mandatory gates and a shared checklist.

  3. Assign accountability

    Make PMs responsible for gate adherence, with design and development accountable for the quality and completeness of their respective inputs.

  4. Observe

    Capture recurring friction, estimation gaps, scope changes, and exceptions through weekly project retrospectives.

  5. Improve

    Translate project-level lessons into changes to the shared workflow, continuously feeding back into prevention.

Handoff is no longer complete when design shares the files. It is complete when engineering accepts them.
